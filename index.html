phyloSophR

An intelligent assistant for phylogenetic comparative research

Dissertation Proposal · In Progress · UMass Boston

Reproducible science shouldn't depend on memory.

phyloSophR is a PhD dissertation project (Objective 1 of "Phylogenetic Comparative Biology: new tools, improving reproducibility, and leveraging its power in other fields") building a tool that turns a plain-language hypothesis into an auditable, preregistered analytical workflow, locked in before any data is touched. Its training data is meant to be built the same way it asks researchers to work: openly, by the community.

The problem

Phylogenetic comparative methods have a documentation gap.

Phylogenetic comparative methods (PCM) have been central to evolutionary biology for four decades, but the field faces two compounding problems, both described in the dissertation proposal's Significance section.

Reproducibility

PCM studies are frequently characterized by long, complex workflows spread across scripts and software: tree pruning, model selection, data transformation, and sensitivity analyses. Standard articles rarely have room to document every decision, and any undocumented choice (a branch-length transformation, a random seed) can subtly change a study's results. Without standardized reporting or pre-registered analytic pipelines, comparative studies are difficult to replicate, verify, or synthesize.

Accessibility

PCM is implemented almost entirely as specialized R libraries that assume programming fluency, statistical modeling knowledge, and English-language documentation, skills that aren't distributed evenly across the scientific community. According to the proposal's cited traffic data, roughly a fifth of visits to a major PCM documentation resource (blog.phytools.org) come from Latin America, Africa, and parts of Asia: regions with some of the world's highest biodiversity, and often the least practical access to the tools needed to study it.

"Reinforcing the analytical foundation of evolutionary biology will have a broad impact across numerous areas of the biological sciences and beyond."

The mechanism

From a hypothesis in plain language to an auditable, preregistered design.

phyloSophR takes a hypothesis stated in natural language, for example "The common ancestor of Australaves should have been a bird of medium size, rather than as large as an ostrich or as small as a New Zealand wren," and walks it through a fixed, versioned pipeline before any data is collected:

  1. 1

    Parse the hypothesis

    A fine-tuned language model extracts the entities and relationships in the hypothesis: trait, taxonomic group, process, predicted direction.

  2. 2

    Assemble the workflow

    Those entities are compiled into a directed acyclic graph (DAG) of analytical operations: an explicit, ordered, back-traceable structure, never a black box.

  3. 3

    Validate, human-in-the-loop

    A logic-checking layer flags structural gaps (like a missing tree-and-trait matching step) and suggests fixes; the researcher decides, never the model.

  4. 4

    Simulate and check power

    The proposed design is stress-tested via simulation across sampled parameter conditions before it's ever run on real data.

  5. 5

    Export, pre-registered

    The finished, resolved design exports as an annotated R script, a Quarto report, and a JSON-LD workflow, ready for an OSF pre-registration.

Because the design is locked in before results exist, it becomes structurally resistant to p-hacking and HARKing (Hypothesizing After Results are Known). This is the central innovation the proposal names for this objective: making good scientific practice the path of least resistance, without adding to a researcher's workload.

The community annotation task

phyloSophR's own training data is meant to be built in the open.

Scientists who work with mined or scraped data know that cleaning it can consume up to 80% of a project's time. Building a gold-standard, domain-specific text corpus faces the same problem, and it shouldn't be done by one person.

What "annotation" means here

To teach a language model to recognize PCM-specific concepts, someone has to read real methods sections and mark up what's in them: which model was fit, on what kind of tree, under which assumptions. Here's a real example from the proposal: a single sentence, annotated.

Phase one: a small expert team

The initial corpus, drawn from 500 peer-reviewed articles spanning the five main PCM analysis types, will be double-annotated by a team of three annotators with varying levels of PCM expertise, using Prodigy's active-learning tooling to prioritize the most informative texts and reduce annotation effort over time. Agreement between annotators will be measured with Cohen's Kappa and Krippendorff's Alpha before any annotated data is released.

Phase two: the community, annotating for the community

A team of three won't be enough to reach the scale a useful corpus needs. Beyond the initial dataset, the proposal describes opening annotation to the wider PCM community — researchers contributing by annotating their own publications, and student volunteers contributing more broadly. The idea is explicit in the proposal's own words:

From the proposal

"This collaborative approach will promote and reinforce the importance of transparency and reproducibility in science, and, at the same time, the training corpus could grow progressively... the annotation task would be a more valuable activity, since it would be work done by the community for the community."

The proposal cites the Smithsonian Institution's Neotropical Studies model — volunteers contributing to documentation and museum-label transcription — as the precedent for this idea, including the possibility of community certificates or acknowledgment in dataset documentation for contributors.

Current status

The community-annotation phase is planned, not active yet. The dissertation proposal has not been defended, the initial expert-annotated corpus doesn't exist yet, and there is no annotation tool or sign-up process to join today. This page exists to explain the idea clearly enough that, when that phase opens, you'll already understand what you'd be signing up for.
